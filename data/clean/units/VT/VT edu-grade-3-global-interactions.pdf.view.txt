u2 Grade 3 – Global Interactions
u3 Overview
u4 In grade 3, students will expand on their understanding of the concept of community and widen their global lens by exploring interactions between groups of people in Africa, Asia, Europe, and Oceania. Students will explain continuities and differences among cultures, governments, traditions, and resources to investigate how individuals work together while acknowledging different perspectives and cultivating empathy. Students will learn how culture spreads across borders, and students will compare and contrast Vermont/local community customs and traditions to others in the world. Students will explain current and historic patterns of human settlement and compare the ways various cultural groups connect and interact. Students will describe how resources vary across geography and climates and discuss how resources can be categorized as renewable and non-renewable.
u5 Looking Back
u6 In grade 2, students expanded their understanding of the concept of community by exploring past and present neighboring communities in Central America, North America, South America, and the Caribbean to explain continuities and differences among cultures, governments, traditions, and available resources.
u7 Looking Ahead
u8 In grade 4, students will examine change and continuity in Vermont and United States history from pre-colonial times through the Civil War.
u9 Inquiry
u10 Why inquiry? Using inquiry as an instructional model for the teaching of social studies allows students to authentically ask questions, search for and analyze evidence, and provide explanations and arguments to challenges facing their world.

u12 The inquiry instructional model is driven by authentic questions or problems that have relevance for children. Students learn content, skills, and dispositions as they seek answers to questions and as they apply their learning to take informed action. Pioneered by Dewey (1938), inquiry is an attempt to make classroom experiences feel more like the authentic, curiosity-driven experiences that motivate learning outside of school. (Using Inquiry to Prepare Students for College, Career, and Civic Life, elementary edition, p. 22)
u13 The Inquiry Arc is defined in Dimension 1 (questions), Dimension 3 (evidence), and Dimension 4 (communication) of the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards. In the AOE-developed Proficiency- Based Learning Social Studies Hierarchy, Dimensions 1, 2, and 3, as well as evidence indicators found within Dimension 2 – History, have been combined into a singular Critical Proficiency – the Inquiry Process. Opportunities for showing proficiency in the Inquiry Process fall within the K-2, 3-5, 6-8 grade bands of the Priority Performance Indicators (below): Constructing Compelling and Supporting Questions, Gathering and Evaluating Sources, and Communicating and Critiquing Solutions. The Inquiry Process Critical Proficiency, as with all of the CPs, begins its focus in the elementary grades and spans through high school, preparing students to attain proficiency in social studies for college and career readiness.
u14 The Critical Proficiency (CP) remains the same K-12 and identifies the enduring skills and knowledge that all students need to demonstrate (across contexts) to be prepared for future learning, including post-secondary career and college readiness. Priority Performance Indicators clarify what will be taught and assessed at each grade cluster.
u15 Critical Proficiency (K-12): Students act as social scientists as they ask questions, think critically, solve problems, and share conclusions.

u213 [T1 R2 C3] Adaptations and Modifications Africa • Adaptation: Nomadic herders in the Sahel move seasonally to find grazing land for animals. • Modification: Building irrigation systems along the Nile River to grow crops in dry areas. Europe • Adaptation: In colder regions like Scandinavia, people wear heavy clothing, build insulated homes, and rely on indoor heating. • Modification: The Dutch built dikes and canals to control flooding and reclaim land from the sea in the Netherlands. Oceania • Adaptation: Indigenous groups in Australia adapted to desert life by learning to find water and hunt with traditional tools. • Modification: People in New Zealand cleared forests for sheep farming and built cities in coastal areas.

u297 Ancient India - Core Knowledge: This 6-lesson unit introduces students to the early Asian civilization of ancient India. Students will explore the geography of India, the Ganges and Indus Rivers, and the development of the first civilization in the Indus valley, where present-day western India, Pakistan, and northeastern Afghanistan are located. The importance of settling near a river to be able to farm near a relatively reliable source of water is also reinforced. In the unit’s six chapters, students will study the culture and beliefs associated with ancient India. They will learn about the Aryan invaders, who came to the Indus valley from other parts of Asia, and whose beliefs became interwoven with those of the Indus people, resulting in the development of a new religion called Hinduism. Students will investigate three important Hindu deities, Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva; the most important Hindu holy books, the Vedas; the castes, or different social levels, that Hindus have divided themselves into; and Diwali, the Hindu festival of lights. Students will also explore Buddhism, which began in this region as well. They will learn about the Buddha, what he taught, and how Buddhism spread to other lands. They will study one of the Buddha’s Jataka tales and how such stories helped him spread his teachings, and they will also examine the life of King Asoka, the ancient king who put Buddha’s teachings into practice.
u298 The Vikings - Core Knowledge: This 8-lesson unit begins by providing geographical information on Scandinavia, homeland of the Vikings, meaning ‘pirates’ in the early Scandinavian languages—a name they were given because of their brutal raids on many places in Europe. Their expertise as sailors and shipbuilders—and their fierce raiding spirit— enabled them to trade, conquer, and explore over a vast area. Students learn about the voyages of Eric the Red and his son, Leif Eriksson. Students are introduced to Norse mythology, including stories of Odin, Thor, and Loki.
