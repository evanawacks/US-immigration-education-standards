u2 Grade 4 - Vermont, the Early Republic and the U.S.: Pre-Colonial Times through the Civil War
u3 Overview
u4 In grade 4, students will examine change and continuity in Vermont and United States history from pre-colonial times through the Civil War. Students will understand the concept of movement and its impact on people, goods, and ideas; learn how natural resources have influenced economic development; and examine the conflicts and compromises that resulted from migration and settlement. This learning will allow students to understand the tensions and factors that led to the fight for independence, the establishment of the United States, and subsequent Vermont statehood. Students will prepare for their role as responsible and informed citizens by examining the Vermont Constitution and the United States Constitution, including the determinants of power and its effects; exploring the development, structure, and function of the United States government as well as the role of individual rights and responsibilities; examining the choices made by various groups of people on the development of the United States through the Civil War; and learn how events, people, and ideas shaped and influenced our democratic system. Grade 4 provides the framework for further study of Vermont and United States History in Grade 7, civics in Grade 8, and United States History/American Government in high school.
u5 Looking Back
u6 In grade 3, students expanded on their understanding of the concept of community and widened their global lens by exploring Africa, Asia, Europe, and Oceania. Students learned about continuities and differences among cultures, governments, traditions, and resources. Students learned about the idea of culture spreading across borders and were able to see the ways various cultural groups connect and interact.

u11 Inquiry
u12 Why inquiry? Using inquiry as an instructional model for the teaching of social studies allows students to authentically ask questions, search for and analyze evidence, and provide explanations and arguments to challenges facing their world.
u13 The inquiry instructional model is driven by authentic questions or problems that have relevance for children. Students learn content, skills, and dispositions as they seek answers to questions and as they apply their learning to take informed action. Pioneered by Dewey (1938), inquiry is an attempt to make classroom experiences feel more like the authentic, curiosity-driven experiences that motivate learning outside of school. (Using Inquiry to Prepare Students for College, Career, and Civic Life, elementary edition, p. 22)
u14 The Inquiry Arc is defined in Dimension 1 (questions), Dimension 3 (evidence), and Dimension 4 (communication) of the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards. In the AOE-developed Proficiency- Based Learning Social Studies Hierarchy, Dimensions 1, 2, and 3, as well as evidence indicators found within Dimension 2 – History, have been combined into a singular Critical Proficiency – the Inquiry Process. Opportunities for showing proficiency in the Inquiry Process fall within the K-2, 3-5, 6-8 grade bands of the Priority Performance Indicators (below): Constructing Compelling and Supporting Questions, Gathering and Evaluating Sources, and Communicating and Critiquing Solutions. The Inquiry Process Critical Proficiency, as with all of the CPs, begins its focus in the elementary grades and spans through high school, preparing students to attain proficiency in social studies for college and career readiness.
u15 The Critical Proficiency (CP) remains the same K-12 and identifies the enduring skills and knowledge that all students need to demonstrate (across contexts) to be prepared for future learning, including post-secondary career and college readiness. Priority Performance Indicators clarify what will be taught and assessed at each grade cluster.
u16 Critical Proficiency (K-12): Students act as social scientists as they ask questions, think critically, solve problems, and share conclusions.

u235 [T1 R2 C3] Conflicts Leading to the American Revolution French and Indian War (1754–1763) • Conflict: Britain and France (with Native allies) fought over land in North America. • Result: Britain won but incurred heavy debt, leading to new taxes on colonists. Proclamation of 1763 • Conflict: Banned colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains to avoid Native conflict. • Colonial response: Colonists resented the restriction on land they felt entitled to after helping in the war. Stamp Act (1765) • Conflict: Required colonists to buy stamps for legal documents and newspapers. • Colonial reaction: "No taxation without representation!" — led to protests, boycotts, and the Stamp Act Congress. Boston Massacre (1770) • Conflict: British soldiers fired into a crowd of colonists, killing five. • Impact: Sparked outrage and used as propaganda by groups like the Sons of Liberty.

u243 [T1 R2 C3] Tea Act and Boston Tea Party (1773) • Conflict: Gave the British East India Company a monopoly on tea sales. • Colonial response: Colonists dumped tea into Boston Harbor in protest. Intolerable Acts (1774) • Conflict: Harsh laws passed to punish Massachusetts for the Tea Party (e.g., closed Boston Harbor). • Effect: United the colonies in opposition; led to the First Continental Congress. Battles of Lexington and Concord (1775) • Conflict: British troops tried to seize colonial weapons; first armed conflict of the revolution. • Effect: “The shot heard 'round the world” — beginning of armed resistance. Compromises (Attempts to Avoid War) Petition to the King (1774) • What: The First Continental Congress asked King George III to repeal the Intolerable Acts. • Result: Ignored by the King, which deepened colonial frustration.

u251 [T1 R2 C3] Olive Branch Petition (1775) • What: Final attempt by the Second Continental Congress to avoid war, pledging loyalty to the Crown but asking for peace. • Result: Rejected by King George III, who declared the colonies in open rebellion. Declaratory Act (1766) (after repeal of Stamp Act) • What: Parliament repealed the unpopular Stamp Act but declared it still had full authority to govern the colonies. • Result: Temporarily calmed tensions but reminded colonists that Britain still claimed power over them. Pre-Civil War Conflicts and Compromises Slavery in New Territories • Conflict: As the United States expanded west, debates erupted over whether new states should allow slavery. • Example: Bleeding Kansas (1854–1856) – Violent clashes between pro- and anti-slavery settlers after the Kansas-Nebraska Act allowed residents to vote on slavery.

u259 [T1 R2 C3] Abolitionist Movement vs. Slaveholders • Conflict: Growing abolitionist voices in the North (like Frederick Douglass, William Lloyd Garrison, Harriet Tubman) called for the end of slavery, while Southern leaders defended it as essential. • Example: Publication of Uncle Tom’s Cabin (1852) by Harriet Beecher Stowe stirred anti-slavery sentiment and angered the South. States’ Rights vs. Federal Power • Conflict: Southern states believed they had the right to govern themselves, especially on issues like slavery and tariffs, while the federal government increasingly intervened. • Example: Nullification Crisis (1832–1833) – South Carolina tried to nullify federal tariffs; President Jackson responded with threats of force. Dred Scott Decision (1857) • Conflict: Supreme Court ruled that African Americans could not be United States citizens and that Congress couldn’t ban slavery in the territories. Impact: Angered Northerners, delighted pro-slavery Southerners, and deepened sectional divides. Missouri Compromise (1820) • What: Missouri entered as a slave state, Maine as a free state; slavery banned in territories north of the 36°30′ line. • Goal: Maintain balance between free and slave states. • Result: Temporarily calmed tensions.
